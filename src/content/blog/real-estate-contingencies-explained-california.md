Contingencies are the part of a purchase contract that most buyers sign without fully understanding, and the part that determines whether a deal going wrong costs you nothing or costs you your deposit.

A contingency is simply a **condition that must be satisfied for the contract to proceed.** While it's in place, you have a defined path to withdraw and recover your earnest money. Once removed, that path closes.

## The three that matter most

| Contingency | Protects you against | If it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inspection | Condition problems you couldn't see | Renegotiate, request repairs or credits, or withdraw |
| Appraisal | The home valuing below your contract price | Renegotiate, cover the gap in cash, or withdraw |
| Loan | Financing falling through | Withdraw, or resolve with the lender |

Each addresses a different risk. Waiving one does not affect the others.

## The inspection contingency

This gives you a defined period to investigate the property's condition and decide whether to proceed.

**What to inspect in Orange County.** A general home inspection is the baseline. Given how much of the county's stock dates to the 1960s and 1970s, specialized inspections are frequently warranted:

- **Sewer lateral** — a camera inspection of the line to the main. Root intrusion and collapsed clay pipe are common in older neighborhoods and expensive to remedy.
- **Roof** — remaining life, not just current leaks
- **Chimney**, on older properties
- **Pest / wood-destroying organisms** — termites and dry rot
- **Foundation**, where the general inspection raises concern

**This period is a second negotiation, and buyers routinely forget they have leverage.** After inspections you can typically request repairs, a credit, or a price reduction. A credit is often preferable to seller-performed repairs — you control the quality and the contractor rather than inheriting the cheapest fix that satisfies the letter of the agreement.

**Read the disclosures during this window too.** The seller's Transfer Disclosure Statement, Natural Hazard Disclosure, any Mello-Roos notice, and HOA documents. For an HOA property, the reserve study and 12 months of meeting minutes are where pending special assessments surface — see [Mello-Roos and HOA fees](/blog/mello-roos-hoa-fees-orange-county).

## The appraisal contingency

Your lender will only finance against the appraised value. If the appraisal comes in below your contract price, a gap opens between what you agreed to pay and what the lender will lend.

Suppose you contract at $1,000,000 with 20% down and the appraisal returns $950,000. The lender bases its loan on $950,000, leaving a $50,000 shortfall. Your options generally:

1. **Cover the difference in cash**, on top of your down payment
2. **Renegotiate** the price with the seller
3. **Split the gap**
4. **Dispute the appraisal**, supplying additional comparable sales — sometimes successful, often not
5. **Withdraw**, if the contingency is still in place

**Waiving the appraisal contingency means committing to option 1 regardless of the shortfall.** In competitive conditions buyers do this to strengthen offers. Only do it if you genuinely have the cash and have decided in advance how large a gap you'd absorb.

## The loan contingency

This protects you if your financing ultimately fails. Pre-approval is not a guarantee — final underwriting can still decline, and circumstances change.

The most common late-stage failures:

- **New credit opened during escrow** — a car loan, a store card, a large purchase
- **Job or income changes**, including moving from salaried to contract work
- **Large undocumented deposits** appearing in your accounts
- **Property-level issues**, such as an HOA that doesn't meet lender requirements or unpermitted work affecting the appraisal

The rule for the entire escrow period is simple: **change nothing about your financial life until you have keys.** This is covered further in [the Orange County home buying timeline](/blog/orange-county-home-buying-timeline).

## Removing contingencies

In California, contingency removal is generally **active** — the buyer delivers a written removal rather than the protection expiring silently on a date. If you need more time, that's a conversation with the seller before the deadline, not after.

**Removal is the point of no return.** Once contingencies are removed, backing out generally puts your earnest money deposit at risk, and the deposit on an Orange County purchase is a significant sum.

Practical discipline:

- **Don't remove a contingency before its purpose is satisfied.** Don't release the loan contingency on verbal reassurance — wait for the lender's actual clearance.
- **Track the dates.** They're short and they run from acceptance.
- **Get extensions in writing** if inspections or underwriting run long.

## Should you waive contingencies to win a home?

Waiving makes an offer stronger and materially riskier. It's a trade, and the right answer depends on conditions and on your finances.

In markets with scarce inventory and multiple offers on everything, buyers waive to compete. When inventory is rising and homes are sitting longer, the pressure to waive is genuinely lower — which is why watching current conditions in the [market report](/blog/orange-county-housing-market-report) matters before deciding.

If you do consider waiving:

- **Inspection** — at minimum, inspect before writing the offer if the seller permits it. Waiving without ever inspecting means accepting unknown condition on a major purchase.
- **Appraisal** — only with cash set aside and a predetermined limit on the gap you'll cover.
- **Loan** — the riskiest to waive. Without it, financing failure can cost your deposit.

There are also ways to strengthen an offer that don't involve accepting risk: a larger deposit, a shorter but not absent inspection period, flexibility on the close date, or a rent-back for a seller who needs time. Terms often matter to a seller more than the last few thousand dollars of price.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a contingency in a real estate contract?

A condition that must be satisfied for the purchase to proceed. While a contingency is in place, the buyer generally has a contractual right to withdraw and recover their earnest money deposit if that specific condition is not met. The three most common cover inspection, appraisal, and loan approval.

### What happens if the appraisal comes in low?

The lender finances against the appraised value rather than the contract price, creating a gap. Buyers typically cover the difference in cash, renegotiate the price, split the shortfall, dispute the appraisal with additional comparable sales, or withdraw if the appraisal contingency remains in place.

### Can I back out of a home purchase in California?

Generally yes while your contingencies remain active, using the terms each provides. After contingency removal, withdrawing typically places your earnest money deposit at risk. Because contract terms vary, review your specific agreement and consult an attorney for questions about your situation.

### What inspections should I get when buying an older home?

A general inspection at minimum. For Orange County's substantial 1960s and 1970s stock, sewer lateral camera inspection, roof, chimney, and pest inspections are frequently worthwhile. Foundation inspection is warranted when the general inspection raises concerns.

### Is it risky to waive the inspection contingency?

Yes. Waiving means accepting the property's condition without the contractual right to renegotiate or withdraw based on what an inspection reveals. If you are considering it, inspect before writing the offer where the seller allows it, so you are at least making an informed decision.

### How long do contingency periods last?

Periods are negotiated in the contract and run from acceptance, typically a matter of days to a few weeks depending on the contingency and the transaction. In California, removal is generally an affirmative act requiring written delivery rather than automatic expiration.

### What is earnest money and can I lose it?

A deposit made after acceptance, held by the neutral escrow holder, demonstrating serious intent. It is generally refundable if you withdraw under an active contingency, and generally at risk if you withdraw after contingencies have been removed.
