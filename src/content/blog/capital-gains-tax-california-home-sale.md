This is the question long-time Orange County homeowners ask most, and it's the one where bad information circulates most freely. What follows describes how the rules generally work. It is not tax advice, and the amounts involved here are large enough that a CPA is genuinely worth the fee.

## The Section 121 exclusion

When you sell your main home at a gain, federal law allows you to exclude:

- **$250,000** if you file as single
- **$500,000** if you're married filing jointly

To qualify you must have **owned** the home and **used** it as your main home for periods totaling **at least two years out of the five years** immediately before the sale. The two years need not be continuous. You can generally claim the exclusion only **once every two years**.

**California conforms to this exclusion**, so it applies on your state return as well.

## Why Orange County sellers can exceed it

In much of the country, $500,000 of gain covers nearly everyone. Here it frequently doesn't — and that catches long-time owners off guard.

Consider a couple who bought decades ago and has watched Orange County values rise substantially since. Their gain can exceed $500,000 comfortably, and the excess is taxable.

The arithmetic, simplified:

```
Sale price
− selling costs (commission, escrow, title, etc.)
= amount realized

Amount realized
− adjusted cost basis
= capital gain

Capital gain − exclusion = taxable gain
```

The single most valuable thing you can do is **make your adjusted basis as high as it legitimately is**, because every dollar of basis is a dollar of gain that never existed.

## What raises your cost basis

Your basis starts at what you paid, and it increases with **capital improvements** — work that adds value, prolongs the home's life, or adapts it to new uses.

Commonly qualifying:

- Room additions, and finishing previously unfinished space
- Kitchen and bathroom remodels
- New roof, HVAC system, water heater
- Windows, permanent landscaping, driveways, fences
- Electrical and plumbing upgrades
- Pools and permanent outdoor structures

Generally **not** qualifying — these are repairs and maintenance:

- Repainting, patching, fixing a leak
- Routine servicing
- Anything replacing a component without meaningfully upgrading it

Certain **selling costs** and some purchase costs also affect the calculation.

**This is why receipts matter enormously.** Thirty years of improvements on an Orange County home can amount to a very large basis adjustment — but only if you can substantiate it. Homeowners who discarded records routinely pay tax on gain they didn't actually have.

If you're years away from selling, start a file now. If you're selling soon, reconstruct what you can: contractor invoices, permits pulled with the city, credit card and bank records, even dated photographs. Permit records are often retrievable from the city even when your own copies are gone.

## How the tax applies above the exclusion

Gain above the exclusion is generally taxed as **long-term capital gain** federally, assuming you held the property more than a year — at 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on your taxable income. Higher-income sellers may also owe the **3.8% Net Investment Income Tax**.

**California is where sellers are most often surprised.** California does not have a separate, preferential capital gains rate. It taxes capital gains as **ordinary income**, at rates reaching **13.3%** for high earners.

So a large taxable gain in California can face federal capital gains tax, potentially the NIIT, and state tax at ordinary rates — simultaneously. That combined exposure is precisely why planning ahead of a large sale is worth professional attention.

## Situations that change the answer

**You didn't live there two of the last five years.** Partial exclusions may be available for certain circumstances — a work-related move, health reasons, or specified unforeseen events. The rules are specific.

**It was a rental at some point.** Depreciation you claimed (or were entitled to claim) is generally recaptured and taxed, separately from the exclusion. Periods of non-qualified use can also reduce the excludable portion.

**You inherited the property.** Inherited property generally receives a basis adjustment to value at the date of death, which frequently eliminates most or all gain on a subsequent sale. This is a very different calculation from a home you purchased. Note also that [Proposition 19](/blog/proposition-19-california-property-tax-transfer) changed the *property tax* treatment of inherited homes — a separate matter from income tax, and one worth understanding alongside it.

**Divorce, death of a spouse, or a change in filing status.** Each affects which exclusion amount applies and how ownership and use periods are counted. A surviving spouse may qualify for the $500,000 exclusion within a limited window after a spouse's death.

**You're selling an investment property.** The Section 121 exclusion applies to a main home, not an investment property. A [1031 exchange](/blog/orange-county-investment-property) may allow deferral instead, with strict identification and closing deadlines.

**You have partial business use**, such as a home office claimed over years. This can affect both the exclusion and depreciation recapture.

## What to do before you sell

1. **Talk to a CPA before listing**, not after closing. Once the sale records, most planning options are gone.
2. **Assemble your improvement documentation.** Invoices, permits, financial records, photographs.
3. **Confirm your ownership and use periods.** Exact dates matter for the two-of-five-year test.
4. **Disclose prior rental or business use** to your tax advisor — it changes the calculation materially.
5. **Ask about timing.** Which tax year a sale falls in can matter, particularly if your income varies.
6. **Estimate the liability before you commit** to your next purchase, so the number doesn't arrive as a surprise while you're in escrow on another home.

If you're 55 or older and moving within California, [Proposition 19](/blog/proposition-19-california-property-tax-transfer) is a separate benefit worth understanding — it addresses your ongoing property tax rather than income tax on the sale, and the two are frequently confused.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much capital gains tax do I pay when selling my home in California?

You may exclude up to $250,000 of gain if filing single, or $500,000 if married filing jointly, provided you owned and used the home as your main home for at least two of the five years before the sale. California conforms to this exclusion but taxes gain above it as ordinary income at rates up to 13.3%, in addition to federal capital gains tax.

### What is the two-out-of-five-year rule?

To qualify for the exclusion, you must have owned the home and used it as your main home for periods totaling at least two years within the five years immediately preceding the sale. The two years need not be continuous, and the exclusion may generally be claimed only once every two years.

### Does California have a separate capital gains tax rate?

No. California taxes capital gains as ordinary income, with rates reaching 13.3% for high earners. There is no preferential state rate for long-term gains, which is why California sellers with large gains often face a higher combined burden than they anticipate.

### What home improvements increase my cost basis?

Capital improvements that add value, prolong the home's life, or adapt it to new uses: additions, kitchen and bath remodels, new roof or HVAC, windows, permanent landscaping, electrical and plumbing upgrades. Routine repairs and maintenance such as repainting or fixing a leak generally do not qualify. Retain receipts and permits, since substantiation is required.

### Do I pay capital gains tax if I buy another house?

Buying a replacement home does not by itself defer or eliminate capital gains tax on your main home — that rule was replaced by the current exclusion. The Section 121 exclusion applies regardless of whether you purchase again. A 1031 exchange can defer gain, but it applies to investment property, not a primary residence.

### What if my home was a rental before I lived in it?

Depreciation claimed or claimable during the rental period is generally recaptured and taxed separately from the exclusion, and periods of non-qualified use can reduce the excludable portion of your gain. This materially changes the calculation, so disclose any prior rental use to your tax advisor.

### Is capital gains tax different for inherited property?

Generally yes. Inherited property typically receives a basis adjustment to its value at the date of death, which often eliminates most or all gain on a later sale. This differs substantially from property you purchased. Separately, Proposition 19 changed the property tax treatment of inherited homes, which is a different issue from income tax.
