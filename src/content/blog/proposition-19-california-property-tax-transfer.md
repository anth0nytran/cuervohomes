There is a particular Orange County homeowner I talk to often: they bought decades ago, the house is now far larger than they need, and they would genuinely like to move. They don't, because they assume moving means trading a 1990s property tax assessment for one based on today's values.

For homeowners 55 and older, Proposition 19 is specifically designed to remove that obstacle. It is widely misunderstood, so here is how it actually works.

## What does Proposition 19 do?

Prop 19 allows a homeowner who is **55 or older** to transfer the *taxable value* of their principal residence to a replacement principal residence.

"Taxable value" means your **factored base year value** — your original Prop 13 assessment plus the small inflationary adjustments allowed since. For a long-held home, that number is usually far below current market value, and that gap is exactly what you're protecting.

The core provisions:

- The replacement home can be **anywhere in California**, not just your current county
- You may use the benefit **up to three times**
- You must be **55 or older when the original property sells**
- The replacement must be purchased or newly constructed **within two years** of the sale
- Both properties must be your **principal residence**
- Either the sale or the purchase must have occurred **on or after April 1, 2021**

The statewide portability is the genuinely significant change. The predecessors to Prop 19 — Propositions 60 and 90 — limited transfers to your own county or to the handful of counties that had agreed to accept them. That constraint is gone.

The same benefit extends to homeowners who are **severely and permanently disabled**, and to victims of **wildfire or declared natural disasters**, under their own qualifying rules.

## What if the new home costs more?

This is the part people get wrong, and it's the part that makes the benefit far more useful than they assume.

You are **not** limited to buying something cheaper. There is no cap on the replacement property's market value. When the replacement costs more than the original sold for, **only the difference is added** to your transferred taxable value.

Worked through, using round numbers for clarity:

| | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Your current taxable (factored base) value | $300,000 |
| Original home sells for | $1,200,000 |
| Replacement home purchased for | $1,400,000 |
| Difference in market value | $200,000 |
| **New taxable value** | **$500,000** |

Without Prop 19, that replacement home would be assessed at its full $1,400,000 purchase price. With it, you're assessed at $500,000 — the transferred base plus only the upgrade amount.

*Illustrative figures, chosen to show the mechanism. Your actual numbers depend on your assessment and sale price.*

If the replacement costs the **same or less** than the original sold for, your taxable value transfers with no addition at all.

## The two-year window works in both directions

The replacement must be purchased or newly constructed within two years of selling the original. Critically, **it can happen before or after the sale.** Buying your replacement first and selling within the following two years also qualifies.

That flexibility matters practically. Sellers in this situation often worry about being forced into interim housing between transactions. You aren't — provided both events fall inside the two-year span and the other conditions are met.

## What Prop 19 also changed — inherited property

Prop 19 is two changes in one measure, and the second one runs in the opposite direction. It substantially **narrowed** the parent-to-child and grandparent-to-grandchild exclusions from reassessment.

Before Prop 19, a child could inherit a parent's property and keep the low assessed value in a broad range of circumstances, including for rentals and second homes. Now the exclusion is considerably more limited — it generally requires the property to become the child's own **principal residence**, and even then a value cap applies above which partial reassessment occurs.

If you are planning around inherited property, or expect to inherit, **this is genuinely a question for an estate attorney or CPA.** The consequences are large, the rules are detailed, and the change caught many families by surprise. Nothing in this article should substitute for that advice.

## How to claim it

The benefit is **not automatic**. You must file a claim with the county assessor where the replacement property is located.

The practical sequence:

1. Confirm you meet the age, residency, and timing requirements before you transact
2. Talk to the assessor's office in the **replacement** county early, especially if you're moving between counties
3. Keep documentation of both closings, including dates and prices
4. File the claim form with that county assessor — there are filing deadlines, so don't leave it
5. Verify the adjustment appears on your subsequent tax bill

If you're moving within Orange County, that's the [Orange County Assessor](https://www.ocassessor.gov/). If you're leaving the county, it's the assessor where you land.

## Who this actually helps

Prop 19 is most valuable to Orange County homeowners who:

- Bought long ago and hold a **large gap** between assessed and market value
- Are **55 or older** and considering downsizing, relocating within California, or moving nearer family
- Have been staying put **primarily because of the tax consequence** rather than because the home still suits them

If your home was purchased recently, the benefit is small — your assessed value is already close to market, so there's little to protect.

There's a second-order effect worth naming: this is one of the few mechanisms that unlocks larger, long-held Orange County homes for the market. For sellers it removes a real barrier; for buyers looking for established properties, it's part of why such homes come available at all.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I transfer my property tax base to a new home in California?

If you are 55 or older, yes. Proposition 19 allows you to transfer the taxable value of your principal residence to a replacement principal residence anywhere in California, up to three times. The replacement must be purchased or newly constructed within two years of selling the original, and both must be your principal residence.

### How many times can I use Proposition 19?

Up to three times for homeowners 55 and older. The prior rules under Propositions 60 and 90 generally allowed a single transfer and restricted it to your own county or a limited set of participating counties.

### What happens if my new home costs more than my old one?

You can still transfer, with no cap on the replacement's value. Only the difference in market value between the two is added to your transferred taxable value. If your base was $300,000, you sold for $1,200,000, and bought for $1,400,000, your new taxable value would be roughly $500,000 rather than the full purchase price.

### Do I have to buy the new home before selling the old one?

No — the order is flexible. The replacement may be purchased or newly constructed either before or after the sale, as long as both fall within a two-year span and the other requirements are satisfied.

### Does Proposition 19 apply if I move out of Orange County?

Yes, anywhere within California. Statewide portability was one of the measure's principal changes. File your claim with the assessor in the county where the replacement property is located.

### How did Proposition 19 change inherited property?

It significantly narrowed the parent-to-child and grandparent-to-grandchild reassessment exclusions. Retaining a low assessed value now generally requires the property to become the heir's principal residence, and a value cap applies above which partial reassessment occurs. Because the consequences for estate planning are substantial, consult an estate attorney or CPA.

### Is the property tax transfer automatic when I buy?

No. You must file a claim with the county assessor for the replacement property, and filing deadlines apply. Contact that assessor's office early — particularly for a move between counties — so the paperwork is handled correctly.
