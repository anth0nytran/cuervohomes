Escrow is the stage where people feel least in control, mostly because nobody explains what's happening. Here's the whole process, roughly in order.

## What is escrow, actually?

Escrow is a **neutral third party** that holds funds and documents and releases them only when every agreed condition has been satisfied.

It exists because a purchase involves a chicken-and-egg problem: the buyer doesn't want to hand over money before receiving clear title, and the seller doesn't want to transfer title before receiving money. The escrow holder takes both sides, verifies that all conditions are met, and completes the exchange simultaneously.

The escrow holder **represents neither party.** They don't advocate for you and can't give legal advice. Their job is to follow the written instructions both parties agreed to — which is precisely why written instructions and amendments matter so much.

## Week one: opening and inspections

**Escrow opens** when the signed contract is delivered to the escrow holder. They assign an escrow number and issue instructions. Read them and confirm names, figures, and dates are correct — errors caught now are trivial and expensive later.

**Earnest money is deposited** with the escrow holder, not with the seller.

**Inspections begin immediately.** The inspection period is short and starts running from acceptance, so schedule on day one rather than day five. A general inspection at minimum, plus specialized inspections where the property warrants — sewer lateral, roof, chimney, and pest are all common on Orange County's substantial 1960s and 1970s housing stock.

**Disclosures arrive.** The seller's Transfer Disclosure Statement, Natural Hazard Disclosure, any Mello-Roos notice, and HOA documents where applicable. Read them properly. For an HOA property, the reserve study and 12 months of meeting minutes are where pending special assessments surface — the reasoning is in [Mello-Roos and HOA fees](/blog/mello-roos-hoa-fees-orange-county).

**The preliminary title report** is issued, showing recorded liens, easements, and restrictions. Review it. An easement across the yard you planned to build on is far better discovered now.

## Week two: negotiation round two

Inspection findings frequently trigger a second negotiation, and buyers often forget they have leverage here.

You can typically request repairs, a credit, or a price reduction. **A credit is frequently better than seller-performed repairs** — you control the contractor and the quality, rather than inheriting the cheapest work that technically satisfies the agreement.

Meanwhile:

- **The appraisal is ordered** by the lender
- **Loan underwriting proceeds**, and you should expect additional document requests, sometimes repeatedly and sometimes for things you've already sent. Respond quickly; this is the most common source of avoidable delay.

If the appraisal comes in below contract price, you're into the options covered in [contingencies explained](/blog/real-estate-contingencies-explained-california): cover the gap in cash, renegotiate, split it, dispute with additional comps, or withdraw if that contingency remains active.

## Week three: contingency removal

As each condition is satisfied, contingencies come off. In California this is generally an **affirmative act** — the buyer delivers written removal rather than protection lapsing silently on a date.

**This is the point of no return.** After removal, withdrawing generally puts the earnest money deposit at risk, and on an Orange County purchase that's a significant sum.

Don't remove a contingency until its purpose is genuinely satisfied. Specifically, don't release the loan contingency on a verbal reassurance — wait for the lender's actual clearance. If you need more time, request an extension in writing **before** the deadline.

## Week four: closing

**Loan documents arrive** at escrow. You'll sign with a notary, frequently a mobile notary who comes to you. Expect a substantial stack, and read the Closing Disclosure against the Loan Estimate you were given — the figures should reconcile.

**Final walkthrough**, typically shortly before closing. Verify that agreed repairs were completed and that the property's condition hasn't changed since you were last inside. Confirm appliances and systems still work and that anything meant to convey is still there.

**Funding.** Your lender wires loan proceeds and you wire your remaining down payment and closing costs.

**Recording.** The deed is recorded with the Orange County Clerk-Recorder. **This is the moment ownership transfers** — not when you signed, not when funds moved. Keys typically follow recording, per the contract terms.

## Wire fraud — the one thing to be paranoid about

This deserves its own section because the losses are catastrophic and usually unrecoverable.

Criminals monitor real estate transactions and send convincing emails, appearing to come from your escrow or title company, containing altered wiring instructions. The email may reference real details about your transaction. Funds sent to a fraudulent account are frequently gone permanently.

**Protect yourself:**

- **Call to verify wiring instructions before every transfer**, using a phone number you obtained independently — from the company's website or your signed documents — never a number in the email itself
- **Be immediately suspicious of any last-minute change** to wiring instructions. Legitimate changes are rare; fraudulent ones are common.
- **Confirm receipt by phone** after sending
- Treat urgency as a red flag rather than a reason to hurry

No legitimate escrow company will object to a verification call.

## What derails escrows at the end

Nearly always one of four things:

**New credit.** Financing a car, opening a store card, or making a large purchase changes your debt-to-income ratio. Lenders re-verify before funding, and this is the most common late-stage failure.

**Job or income changes.** Changing employers, moving from salaried to contract work, or a drop in commission income can undo an approval.

**Large undocumented deposits.** Money entering your accounts without a paper trail triggers underwriting scrutiny. Document gifts properly and early.

**Appraisal shortfalls**, as above.

The rule for the entire escrow period is simple: **change nothing about your financial life until you have keys.** Sellers buying simultaneously are subject to exactly the same rule on their purchase.

## Who pays for what

Cost allocation is **negotiable and set by the contract**, not fixed by law, and local custom varies. Commonly:

| Item | Customarily paid by |
| --- | --- |
| Escrow fee | Often split, per contract |
| Owner's title insurance | Negotiated; varies by county custom |
| Lender's title insurance | Buyer |
| Loan fees and points | Buyer |
| County transfer tax | Commonly seller |
| Property tax and HOA dues | Prorated between the parties |
| Home warranty | Negotiated |

Your escrow holder will provide an estimated settlement statement. Review it early rather than at signing, and ask about anything you don't recognize.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does escrow take in California?

Typically 30 to 45 days for a financed purchase, driven largely by the buyer's lender. Cash purchases can close considerably faster since no appraisal or loan underwriting is required. The timeline is set in the contract and can be extended by written agreement.

### What does an escrow company do?

It acts as a neutral third party holding funds and documents, verifying that all agreed conditions are satisfied, and completing the exchange of money and title simultaneously. It represents neither buyer nor seller and cannot provide legal advice — it follows the written instructions both parties agreed to.

### When do I actually own the house?

At recording, when the deed is recorded with the county. Signing loan documents and even funding do not by themselves transfer ownership. Keys are typically released following recording, according to the contract terms.

### What is a final walkthrough?

A visit shortly before closing to confirm that agreed repairs were completed, that the property's condition has not changed since you last saw it, and that items meant to convey with the sale are still present. It is not a new inspection, but it is your last opportunity to raise a problem before closing.

### Can escrow fall through?

Yes. The most common causes are the buyer opening new credit or changing jobs during escrow, large undocumented deposits triggering underwriting problems, appraisals below contract price, and inspection findings that reopen negotiation without resolution. Most are avoidable with discipline and early communication.

### How do I avoid wire fraud when buying a home?

Always call to verify wiring instructions before every transfer, using a phone number obtained independently from the company's website or your signed documents — never a number contained in an email. Treat any last-minute change to wiring instructions as fraudulent until verified by phone, and confirm receipt after sending.

### Who pays escrow fees in California?

Allocation is negotiable and set by the purchase contract, with local custom varying by county. Escrow fees are frequently split, lender's title insurance is typically the buyer's cost, and county transfer tax is commonly the seller's. Your estimated settlement statement will itemize the actual allocation for your transaction.
