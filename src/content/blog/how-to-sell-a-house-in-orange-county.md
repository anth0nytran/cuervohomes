Selling a home is a sequence, and almost every expensive mistake comes from rushing one stage to get to the next. Below is the whole sequence, with honest time ranges and the specific California requirements that catch first-time sellers off guard.

For the current pricing environment — inventory, days on market, and what homes are actually closing for — see the [Orange County housing market report](/blog/orange-county-housing-market-report), which is updated monthly.

## How long does it take to sell a house in Orange County?

Budget **60 to 90 days** from decision to recording, split roughly like this:

| Stage | Typical duration | What controls it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Preparation and pricing | 2–4 weeks | Repairs, decluttering, photography scheduling |
| Active on market | Highly variable | Pricing accuracy, above all |
| Offer to accepted contract | 1–7 days | Negotiation rounds, competing offers |
| Escrow to close | 30–45 days | Buyer financing, inspections, appraisal |

The one stage nobody can promise you is the middle. A correctly priced home in a normal Orange County market moves in weeks; an overpriced one can sit for months and still sell for less than it would have on day one. That is not a sales pitch — it is what the days-on-market spread in the county data shows every single month.

## Stage 1: Preparation and pricing

**Start with a real valuation, not an estimate.** Automated tools can't see condition, upgrades, view, or which side of a school boundary you sit on. In a county where two homes on one block can differ by hundreds of thousands, that gap is the whole game. There's a longer explanation in [how much is my home worth in Orange County](/blog/how-much-is-my-home-worth-orange-county).

**Fix what a buyer will notice; skip what they won't.** The highest-return work is almost always cosmetic and cheap: paint, deep cleaning, decluttering, landscaping, replacing dated light fixtures and hardware. Major renovations rarely return their cost when done purely to sell.

**Deal with deferred maintenance you already know about.** Buyers discount visible neglect by far more than the repair would have cost, and an inspection will surface it anyway — at which point you're negotiating from a weaker position than if you'd handled it up front.

**Photography comes last, after everything is done.** Listing photos are the first showing, and most buyers decide from a phone screen whether your home makes their list.

## Stage 2: Pricing strategy

Price against **recently sold** comparable homes, not against active listings. Active listings tell you what your neighbors *hope* to get. Sold comps tell you what buyers actually paid.

The single most important window in the entire process is the **first two to three weeks**. That is when a new listing gets its peak exposure — saved searches fire, agents preview it, buyers who've been watching the area finally see something new. Priced correctly, that attention converts into offers. Priced 10% high, you spend that window collecting nothing, and by the time you reduce, the listing carries a days-on-market number that buyers read as a problem.

Reducing later almost never recovers the momentum you gave away. This is why "let's try a higher number and see" is the most expensive sentence in real estate.

## Stage 3: Marketing and showings

Expect professional photography, MLS syndication to the major portals, and targeted digital promotion. Ask any agent you interview exactly what their marketing includes in writing — the range in the industry is enormous.

Practical points during showings:

- **Leave for showings.** Buyers won't speak freely with an owner present, and they need to imagine living there.
- **Expect short notice.** The more accommodating you are, the more showings you get.
- **Secure valuables, medications, and documents** before the first showing.
- **Track feedback for patterns.** One buyer disliking your kitchen is noise. Six mentioning the same thing is data.

## Stage 4: Offers and negotiation

Price is one term among several, and the highest number is not automatically the best offer. Weigh:

- **Financing type and strength.** A verified pre-approval with a solid down payment is meaningfully less risky than a marginal one. Cash removes appraisal and loan risk entirely.
- **Contingencies.** Inspection, appraisal, and loan contingencies are exit ramps. Fewer or shorter means more certainty.
- **Timeline.** Does the close date fit your move? A rent-back can bridge the gap if you need time.
- **Buyer-agent compensation.** Since the 2024 NAR settlement this is negotiated within the transaction rather than advertised on the MLS. See [who pays the real estate agent commission in California](/blog/who-pays-real-estate-agent-commission-california).

You can accept, reject, or counter any offer. Countering on terms rather than price is often what turns a decent offer into the right one.

## Stage 5: What California requires you to disclose

This is where out-of-state sellers are most often surprised. California disclosure obligations are broad, and the governing standard is straightforward: **disclose known material facts affecting value or desirability.** Documents commonly required in a residential resale include:

- **Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS)** — the seller's own account of the property's condition and known defects
- **Natural Hazard Disclosure (NHD)** — whether the property sits in a designated flood, fire, seismic, or other hazard zone
- **Lead-based paint disclosure** — required for homes built before 1978
- **Mello-Roos / special assessment disclosure** — significant across much of Orange County's master-planned housing
- **Earthquake hazards** and other state-specific disclosures depending on the property and its age
- **HOA documents** — governing documents, budget, and financials where an association exists

The strategic point: **over-disclosure protects you, under-disclosure follows you.** Failing to disclose a known material defect can create liability that outlives the closing by years. If you're unsure whether something rises to material, disclose it. Your agent will guide the paperwork, and a real estate attorney is the right call for genuinely borderline questions.

## Stage 6: Escrow

Escrow in California typically runs **30 to 45 days**, driven mostly by the buyer's lender. During it:

1. The buyer deposits earnest money with a neutral escrow holder
2. Inspections happen — general, and often specialized: sewer, roof, chimney, pest
3. The buyer may request repairs or credits based on findings; this is a second negotiation
4. The lender orders an appraisal
5. Title is searched and title insurance issued
6. The buyer removes contingencies as each condition is satisfied
7. Final walkthrough
8. Signing, funding, and recording — you're closed at recording, not at signing

**Where escrows most often stumble:** appraisal below contract price, inspection findings that reopen negotiation, and buyer financing falling through late. An experienced agent anticipates all three rather than reacting to them.

**Keep your own finances still, too.** Sellers sometimes assume the rule only applies to buyers, but if you're purchasing your next home simultaneously, a new credit line can jeopardize *that* loan.

## What sellers most often get wrong

**Pricing on what they need rather than what it's worth.** The market is indifferent to your payoff amount or your next down payment.

**Treating the first two weeks as a trial run.** They aren't. They're the peak.

**Refusing to leave for showings.**

**Under-disclosing to avoid scaring buyers.** This trades a short-term worry for long-term legal exposure.

**Choosing an agent on commission rate alone.** On an Orange County home, a single percentage point is a large number — but a stronger negotiated outcome routinely exceeds the difference in fee. Ask what you get, then decide.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does it take to sell a house in Orange County?

Plan on 60 to 90 days from decision to recording: two to four weeks to prepare and price, a variable marketing period driven mostly by pricing accuracy, and a 30 to 45 day escrow. Correctly priced homes move substantially faster than the county average, and overpriced ones can sit for months.

### What do I have to disclose when selling a house in California?

California requires sellers to disclose known material facts affecting a property's value or desirability. Typical documents include the Transfer Disclosure Statement, Natural Hazard Disclosure, lead-based paint disclosure for pre-1978 homes, Mello-Roos and special assessment disclosures, and HOA documents where applicable. When in doubt, disclose — the liability for omission outlasts the sale.

### Should I make repairs before selling my home?

Make cosmetic repairs and address deferred maintenance you already know about. Paint, cleaning, decluttering, landscaping, and dated fixtures return the most. Major renovations undertaken purely to sell rarely recover their cost. An inspection will surface known defects regardless, so handling them early is usually cheaper than negotiating them later.

### How long does escrow take in California?

Typically 30 to 45 days, largely determined by the buyer's lender. Cash purchases can close considerably faster since no appraisal or loan underwriting is required. The sale is complete at recording, not at signing.

### What happens if the appraisal comes in below the offer price?

The lender will only finance against the appraised value, creating a gap. Common resolutions: the buyer covers the difference in cash, the price is renegotiated, the parties split the shortfall, or the appraisal is disputed with additional comparable sales. Which path is available depends on how the appraisal contingency was written.

### Can I sell my house without an agent in Orange County?

Yes, California permits it. Be aware that you take on pricing, marketing, disclosure compliance, negotiation, and escrow coordination yourself, and that disclosure liability is the same either way. Most sellers who go this route still engage a real estate attorney for the contract and disclosure package.

### Should I sell before or after buying my next home?

It depends on your finances and risk tolerance. Selling first gives certainty about proceeds but may require interim housing or a rent-back. Buying first avoids moving twice but means carrying two payments if your sale takes longer than expected. In a market with rising inventory, the risk of carrying two payments deserves careful thought.
